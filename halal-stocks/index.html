How many Indian stocks are halal?

204 of the 501 companies in the Nifty 500 pass NSE's Shariah screen, as of 30 September 2026 — about 41% of the companies, and about 31% of the index by market value. A company passes if its main business is permissible and its borrowing, interest income and cash-like assets stay under fixed limits; for NSE, the screen is applied by TASIS and reviewed every month. Other screens draw those limits in different places, so a stock can pass one list and fail another.

The number, by companies and by value

Of the 501 companies in the Nifty 500 at the end of September 2026, 204 pass NSE's Shariah screen. Counted by market value instead, the companies that pass make up a smaller share of the index — the companies the screen removes are, on average, the bigger ones.

Sources: Nifty500 Shariah and Nifty 500 factsheets, NSE Indices — as of 30 September 2026. 40.7% is 204 ÷ 501. The market-value share is worked out from Infosys, which is 1.83% of the Nifty 500 and 5.83% of the Shariah index: both indices weight each company by the value of its freely traded shares, so 1.83 ÷ 5.83 is the Shariah index's share of the whole.

Financial services — banks, insurers and lenders — are the Nifty 500's biggest sector, and the screen removes them as a business: they are 30.58% of the Nifty 500 and 0.18% of the Shariah index. What gets a company through

NSE's screen asks two questions, in order. First, the business: a company that is "primarily into" conventional finance, alcohol, pork, tobacco, gambling, narcotic drugs, or entertainment and hospitality of the kinds the methodology names is out, whatever its numbers. Second, the money: a company that passes the first test must also stay under these limits.

  • Interest-based debt: up to 25% of total assets
  • Interest income, plus an assumed 7.5% return on interest-based investments: up to 3% of total income
  • Money owed to the company plus cash and bank balances: up to 90% of total assets

Source: NSE Indices, methodology for the Nifty Shariah indices, which says the screens are provided by TASIS (Taqwaa Advisory and Shariah Investment Solutions) — document dated January 2020, checked 24 September 2026.

AAOIFI, the international body that sets Shariah standards for Islamic finance, measures debt against the company's market value rather than its assets; Dow Jones does too, but averages the market value over 24 months. The methodology page sets the standards side by side, with an example you can change.

What passing does and doesn't mean

It is one screen's list

The 204 are the companies NSE's screen lets through. Apps and other index providers apply their own limits, so their lists differ — usually because the methods differ, not because anyone made a mistake. Why halal stock lists disagree.

It changes every month

NSE reviews its Shariah indices every month and removes any company its Shariah board finds no longer compliant. A company on the list today passed at the last review — not for all time.

It is not a recommendation

Passing says a company's business and balance sheet sit inside the screen's limits on the date checked. It says nothing about whether its shares are worth buying, and this page doesn't either.

Some income still needs purifying

The limits tolerate a small share of interest income; they don't make it clean. NSE's methodology expects investors to give away their share of it. The purification calculator works it out three ways.

Source for the monthly review and for purification: NSE Indices, methodology for the Nifty Shariah indices — checked 24 September 2026.

Where to see the companies

NSE Indices publishes the Nifty500 Shariah index on niftyindices.com, including its full list of companies. The monthly factsheet shows the count, the sector weights and the ten largest holdings.

Of India's six Shariah mutual funds, one tracks this index: the UTI Nifty500 Shariah Index Fund. That is a fact about what the fund follows, not a recommendation — the compare page sets all six side by side.
