Is Sukanya Samriddhi Yojana halal?

Sukanya Samriddhi pays a government-set interest rate on money a parent chooses to deposit — the same structure as PPF. Two Indian bodies have ruled on it specifically: Jamiat Ulama-i-Hind, in a 2019 resolution by around 200 jurists including Darul Uloom Deoband's mufti, declared it against Shariah because it pays interest, and TASIS lists it as Shariah non-compliant for the same reason. The practical catch is that money already in an account keeps earning interest until it matures, 21 years after opening, and the account can only be closed early in specific circumstances.

How Sukanya Samriddhi works

Sukanya Samriddhi is a government savings scheme for a girl child, launched in 2015. A parent opens it in the daughter's name, deposits into it for 15 years, and the government credits a rate it sets every quarter.

How the Sukanya Samriddhi scheme works
How it works Detail
Who opens it A parent or guardian, in the name of a daughter aged up to 10
Accounts per family 2, with more allowed for twins or triplets
Rate 8.2% a year, compounded annually, set by the Ministry of Finance each quarter
Deposits ₹250 minimum and ₹1,50,000 maximum in a financial year, for 15 years
Maturity 21 years from opening — interest keeps being credited until then
Who runs the account The parent or guardian, until the girl turns 18
Taking money out early Up to 50% for higher education once she turns 18 or finishes Class 10; full closure early only in specific cases, such as her marriage after 18
Tax treatment Deduction under Section 80C, tax-free interest, tax-free maturity

Rate as announced for July–September 2026, unchanged from the previous quarter: Upstox, 30 June 2026. Scheme rules: Sukanya Samriddhi Account — as of 23 September 2026.

Like PPF, Sukanya Samriddhi is a small savings scheme, so deposits go into the National Small Savings Fund, which lends them onward to the central and state governments at interest. The PPF page sets out how that works.

What the standards say

Sukanya Samriddhi has been ruled on by name twice — once by a collective of Indian jurists, and once by a Shariah certification body.

Jamiat Ulama-i-Hind — against Shariah, because it pays interest

At a three-day conference reported in March 2019, around 200 Islamic jurists gathered by Jamiat Ulama-i-Hind passed a resolution declaring the scheme against Shariah. The reason given was the interest itself: the balance grows each year by an interest credit. Those named at the conference included Jamiat's then president and three senior figures from Darul Uloom Deoband.

What the resolution says "The bank annually increases the amount by [adding] annual interest up to nine percent. It is because that this scheme involves interest, it is clearly un-Islamic." Those named as attending included Maulana Qari Mohammad Usman Mansurpoori, then president of Jamiat Ulama-i-Hind; Mufti Habiburrahman Khairabadi, mufti of Darul Uloom Deoband; Maulana Nematullah Azmi of Darul Uloom Deoband; and Mufti Saeed Ahmad Palanpuri, Sheikhul Hadith of Darul Uloom Deoband. — Jamiat Ulama-i-Hind resolution, as reported by PTI, 30 March 2019. TASIS — Shariah non-compliant

TASIS, which certifies two of the six Shariah mutual funds on the comparison page, answers the question directly on its own site: investing in Sukanya Samriddhi is Shariah non-compliant, because it is a debt instrument paying a fixed rate of interest.

What TASIS says Asked "Sukanya samridhi yojna is halal or haram and why?", TASIS answers that "investment in Sukanya Samridhi Yojana is Shariah non-compliant", describing it as a debt instrument with fixed interest. Its answer quotes an interest rate of 8.4%, from an earlier quarter — the rate has changed since, but the reasoning does not depend on it. — TASIS, frequently asked questions. Read the answer

How this lines up with PPF

The structure matches what the PPF page sets out: a voluntary deposit of money you already own, lent onward at a contracted return. That is the structure Darul Uloom Deoband treated as interest in its PPF rulings, as opposed to a compulsory provident fund deducted before the wages reach you. No ruling was found holding Sukanya Samriddhi permissible; if you know of one, send it to hello@purevesting.com.

What happens if you stop depositing

This is the part most parents asking the question need, and it is where Sukanya Samriddhi differs from simply not opening an account. Stopping deposits does not stop the interest.

What happens to a Sukanya Samriddhi account when deposits stop
If you… What happens
Miss the ₹250 minimum in a year The account goes into default. It can be revived by paying the missed minimums plus a ₹50 penalty for each year missed.
Keep depositing for the full 15 years Deposits stop after year 15, but interest keeps being credited until year 21.
Want to close it early Only allowed in specific cases, such as the girl's marriage after 18. There is no general option to close on request.
Want part of the money for education Up to 50% can be withdrawn once she turns 18 or finishes Class 10, against actual fees.

Same sources as the scheme table above. Rules are from the Sukanya Samriddhi Account Scheme, 2019, which replaced the 2016 rules.

So for a parent who opened an account years ago, the question is not only whether to keep depositing. It is also what to do about interest that will keep arriving in the daughter's name for years regardless.

What people do in practice

What follows describes approaches people take and the reasoning they give for them. It is not a list of options being recommended, and which one applies to you is a question for your scholar, not for this page.

Not opening an account

Parents who follow the rulings above and have not opened an account save for the same goal some other way, accepting that no Shariah-compliant alternative guarantees a return the way this scheme does.

Keeping the account, giving the interest away

Neither ruling above addresses an account that already exists. Some parents keep it and give the interest to the poor each year — the approach Darul Uloom Deoband set out for PPF. That is an analogy, not a ruling on Sukanya Samriddhi itself.

Paying only the minimum

Keeping the account alive at ₹250 a year avoids default penalties while adding as little new money as the rules allow. It does not reduce the interest already accruing on the balance.

Asking with the daughter's ownership in mind

The account legally belongs to the girl, not the parent. People who take the question to a scholar tend to state that fact up front, because what may be done with a minor's money is its own question.

Your account passbook, from the post office or bank holding it, shows interest credited separately from deposits — the figure the second approach above works from.
