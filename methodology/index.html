How do you check if a stock is halal?

A Shariah screen asks two questions, in order: is the company's main business permissible, and does it borrow and keep its cash mostly without interest? The second question is answered with ratios — AAOIFI, the international body that sets Shariah standards for Islamic finance, allows interest-bearing debt and interest-bearing deposits of up to 30% of the company's market value each, while NSE's Shariah indices, screened by TASIS, allow debt of up to 25% of total assets instead. Because the standards measure against different things, the same company can pass one screen and fail another — which is why halal stock lists disagree.

Step 1: what the company does

The first test looks at the company's main business. If the business itself is prohibited, nothing else matters and the company is out. Here is the list NSE applies to its Shariah indices — the one behind the 205 figure on this site's home page. A company that is, in NSE's words, "primarily into" any of these is screened out:

  • Conventional financial services — banks, insurance companies, finance and investment companies, stock broking
  • Pork, alcohol and tobacco
  • Gambling and narcotic drugs
  • Film and other entertainment "where vulgarity, promiscuity is a part and parcel of the business"
  • Hotels and restaurants providing non-halal products or entertainment

Other standards draw the edges of this list differently. Dow Jones's list, for instance, also names recreational cannabis, and weapons used for purposes other than defence. But conventional finance, alcohol, pork, gambling and tobacco are on both lists.

Note the word primarily: a company whose main business is permissible is not screened out just because a small part of what it does is not. AAOIFI and Dow Jones put a number on how small — under 5% of what the company earns. NSE's methodology document does not give one.

Sources: NSE Indices, methodology for the Nifty Shariah indices — document dated January 2020; S&P Dow Jones Indices, Dow Jones Islamic Market methodology — September 2026 edition. Both checked 24 September 2026.

Step 2: how it borrows and keeps its cash

Almost every company borrows at some point and keeps cash in a bank. A screen that excluded any contact with interest would exclude nearly everything, so the standards set limits instead. This is where they differ most — not only in the number, but in what the number is measured against.

Shariah screening financial limits under AAOIFI Standard 21 and NSE's Shariah indices
Test AAOIFI NSE (TASIS)
Interest-bearing debt Up to 30% of the market value of the company's shares Up to 25% of total assets
Interest-bearing deposits and investments Up to 30% of the market value of the company's shares No separate limit — counted in the income test below
Income from non-permissible sources Under 5% of total income Interest income, plus an assumed 7.5% return on interest-based investments: up to 3% of total income
Cash and money owed to the company No test Receivables plus cash and bank balances: up to 90% of total assets

AAOIFI's own page for Standard 21 currently shows unrelated content, so the AAOIFI limits are quoted from an academic paper that cites the standard. NSE's limits are from NSE's own methodology document.

What the sources say On AAOIFI: "interest-bearing debt and interest-bearing deposits must each not exceed 30% of the firm's market capitalisation, and income from non-permissible activities must remain below 5% of total income." — Qadi, Sharma and Medda, "Beyond Binary Screens: A Continuous Shariah Compliance Index for Asset Pricing and Portfolio Design", arXiv, 2025. Read the paper

On NSE: "NSE Indices Limited has contracted with Taqwaa Advisory and Shariah Investment Solutions (TASIS) to provide the Shariah screens … Interest based-debt should be less than or equal to 25% of Total Assets … Interest income plus returns (currently considered @7.5%) from interest-based investments should be less than or equal to 3% of the total income … Receivables plus cash and bank balances should be less than or equal to 90% of Total Assets." — NSE Indices, methodology for the Nifty Shariah indices. Read the methodology

Where "33%" comes from

If you have seen a 33% limit quoted elsewhere, it is not AAOIFI's number. It is most likely Dow Jones's old one: the Dow Jones Islamic Market indices capped debt at 33% of the company's market value until September 2026.

From 18 September 2026, their methodology sets two limits instead, each under 30%: interest-bearing debt, and cash plus interest-bearing securities. Both are measured against the company's market value averaged over the last 24 months rather than on a single day, which smooths out swings in the share price. Articles, apps and papers written before the change still quote 33%.

Source: S&P Dow Jones Indices, Dow Jones Islamic Market methodology — September 2026 edition, including its record of methodology changes. Checked 24 September 2026.

Try it: one company, two screens

Take a made-up company. Its shares are worth ₹10,000 crore in total, it owns ₹8,000 crore of assets, and it has borrowed ₹2,200 crore at interest. Here is how its debt looks under each standard's yardstick.

Change any of the three numbers and the table below updates.

The example company's debt measured under two Shariah screening standards
Screen This company Result
AAOIFI Debt up to 30% of market value 22.0% ✓ Within
NSE (TASIS) Debt up to 25% of total assets 27.5% ✗ Over

This checks the debt test only. A real screen applies every test in step 2, as well as step 1.

The same ₹2,200 crore of debt is within one limit and over the other. Now change the market value and watch which row moves: AAOIFI's does, NSE's does not. That is the practical difference between the yardsticks — under a market-value test, a falling share price alone can push a company over the limit, even if nothing about the business has changed. NSE's test never looks at the share price.

What happens to the small leftover income

A company that passes the screen can still earn a little from something non-permissible — interest on its bank balance, most often. The limits in step 2 tolerate that small share; they do not make it clean. The investor is expected to give away their proportionate share of it. This is called purification.

What NSE's methodology says "For full compliance with Shariah law, investors are required to purge the pro rata portion of interest income accrued on their holding of shares in a company." — NSE Indices, methodology for the Nifty Shariah indices. Read the methodology

In plain terms: if you own a tiny slice of a company, you are responsible for the same tiny slice of its interest income, and that amount is given to charity without expecting reward for it.

What the screen does to the real market

Of the 500 companies in the Nifty 500, 205 pass NSE's Shariah screen — the figure drawn as squares on the home page. What is left is not a smaller copy of the market. It is a differently shaped one.

Three things stand out. Financial services all but disappears, at 0.19% of the index — step 1 removes banks, insurers and lenders outright. Power barely appears either, at 0.13%. Power is not on step 1's list, so its near-absence comes from the ratio tests in step 2 — power plants are usually built with borrowed money. And what remains leans heavily on a few sectors: the four IT companies in the index's top ten — Infosys, TCS, HCL Technologies and Tech Mahindra — make up 14.39% of it between them.

Why halal stock lists disagree

If one app shows a stock as halal and another does not, the usual reason is not that one of them made a mistake. They are applying different methods, each correctly.

The main reasons Shariah screening providers reach different results
Where methods differ What it changes
What debt is measured against Market value moves with the share price every day; total assets move only when the company reports
The limit itself NSE allows 25%; AAOIFI and Dow Jones allow 30%. On the same yardstick, a company at 27% would pass one and fail the other
Averaging Dow Jones averages market value over 24 months and S&P's Shariah indices over 36; a screen that uses a single day's figure moves with every swing in the share price
Extra tests NSE adds a test on cash plus money owed to the company; Dow Jones adds one on cash plus interest-bearing securities. A company can pass the debt test and fail one of these
How often the list is updated NSE reviews its Shariah indices every month and removes any company its Shariah board finds non-compliant; other providers work to their own schedules, so two lists checked on the same day can reflect different months

Sources: NSE's methodology and the Dow Jones Islamic Market methodology, both linked above, and S&P Dow Jones Indices' methodology for the S&P Shariah indices — September 2026 edition — for the 36-month average. Checked 24 September 2026.

The 205 figure on this site's home page uses NSE's list because the exchange publishes it every month, dated, where anyone can check it. That is a choice about which figure to show, not a ruling that NSE's method is the right one. Which standard to follow is a question for your scholar.
