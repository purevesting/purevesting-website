How do you check if a stock is halal?

A Shariah screen asks two questions: is the company's main business halal, and does it borrow and keep its cash mostly without interest? The international Shariah standard allows debt of up to 30% of the company's market value; NSE allows up to 25% of its total assets. Because they measure against different things, a company can pass one and fail the other — which is why halal stock lists disagree.

Step 1: what the company does

First, the main business. If the business itself is haram, nothing else matters: the company is out. NSE screens out a company that is, in its words, "primarily into" any of these:

  • Conventional financial services — banks, insurance companies, finance and investment companies, stock broking
  • Pork, alcohol and tobacco
  • Gambling and narcotic drugs
  • Film and other entertainment "where vulgarity, promiscuity is a part and parcel of the business"
  • Hotels and restaurants providing non-halal products or entertainment

Other screens draw the edges of this list differently. A big US index company's list, for instance, also names recreational cannabis, and weapons used for purposes other than defence. But conventional finance, alcohol, pork, gambling and tobacco are on both lists.

Note the word primarily: a company whose main business is halal is not screened out just because a small part of what it does is not. The international Shariah standard and that index company put a number on how small — under 5% of what the company earns. NSE's rules don't give one.

Step 2: how it borrows and keeps its cash

Almost every company borrows at some point and keeps cash in a bank. A screen that removed every company touching interest would remove nearly all of them, so the screens set limits instead. This is where they differ most: not only in the number, but in what the number is measured against.

Shariah screening limits under the international Shariah standard and NSE's Shariah indices
Test International standard NSE
Interest-bearing debt Up to 30% of the market value of the company's shares Up to 25% of total assets
Interest-bearing deposits and investments Up to 30% of the market value of the company's shares No separate limit — counted in the income test below
Income from non-permissible sources Under 5% of total income Interest income, plus returns from interest-based investments: up to 2.5% of total income
Cash and money owed to the company No test Receivables plus cash and bank balances: up to 90% of total assets
What the sources say On the international standard: "interest-bearing debt and interest-bearing deposits must each not exceed 30% of the firm's market capitalisation, and income from non-permissible activities must remain below 5% of total income." — A 2025 research paper that cites the standard.

On NSE: "Interest based-debt should be less than or equal to 25% of Total Assets … Interest income plus returns from interest-based investments should be less than or equal to 2.5% of the total income … Receivables plus cash and bank balances should be less than or equal to 90% of Total Assets." — NSE Indices, methodology document for equity indices, September 2026, section 34.
Read more: where "33%" comes from

If you have seen a 33% limit quoted elsewhere, it is not the international standard's number. It is most likely the old limit of a big US index company's Islamic indices, which capped debt at 33% of the company's market value until September 2026.

From 18 September 2026, they use two limits instead, each under 30%: interest-bearing debt, and cash plus interest-bearing securities. Both are measured against the company's market value averaged over the last 24 months, not on a single day, which smooths out swings in the share price. Articles, apps and papers written before the change still quote 33%.

Try it: one company, two screens

Take a made-up company. Its shares are worth ₹10,000 crore in total, it owns ₹8,000 crore of assets, and it has borrowed ₹2,200 crore at interest. Here is how its debt alone looks under each screen.

Change any of the three numbers and the table below updates.

The example company's debt measured under two Shariah screening standards
Screen This company Result
International standard Debt up to 30% of market value 22.0% ✓ Within
NSE Debt up to 25% of total assets 27.5% ✗ Over

The same ₹2,200 crore of debt is within one limit and over the other. Now change the market value and watch which row moves: the international standard's does, NSE's doesn't. That is the real difference — under a market-value test, a falling share price alone can push a company over the limit, even if nothing about the business has changed. NSE's test never looks at the share price.

What happens to the small leftover income

A company that passes can still earn a little from something haram — most often, interest on its bank balance. The limits in step 2 allow that small share; they don't make it clean. You are expected to give away your share of it. This is called purification.

What NSE's rules say "For full compliance with Shariah law, investors are required to purge the pro rata portion of interest income accrued on their holding of shares in a company." — NSE Indices, methodology document for equity indices, September 2026, section 34.

In plain words: if you own a tiny slice of a company, you are responsible for the same tiny slice of its interest income, and you give that amount to charity without expecting reward.

There is more than one way to work out that slice, and the answers can be far apart. The purification calculator shows the three common methods side by side for any company.

What the screen does to the real market

Of the 501 companies in the Nifty 500 at the end of September 2026, 204 pass NSE's Shariah screen — the squares on the home page. What is left is not a smaller copy of the market. It is a differently shaped one.

Share of the Shariah index

Information technology 17.39%
Healthcare 16.06%
Capital goods 14.20%
Fast-moving consumer goods 8.54%
Consumer services 8.41%
The other eleven sectors combined 35.09%
Financial services 0.18%
Power 0.13%

Three things stand out. Financial services all but disappear, at 0.18% of the index — step 1 removes banks, insurers and lenders outright. Power barely appears either, at 0.13%. Power is not on step 1's list, so its near-absence comes from the money tests in step 2: power plants are usually built with borrowed money. And what remains leans heavily on a few sectors: the four IT companies in the index's top ten — Infosys, TCS, HCL Technologies and Tech Mahindra — make up 13.07% of it between them.

Why halal stock lists disagree

If one app shows a stock as halal and another does not, the usual reason is not that one of them made a mistake. They are using different methods, each correctly.

The main reasons Shariah screens reach different results
Where methods differ What it changes
What debt is measured against Market value moves with the share price every day; total assets move only when the company reports
The limit itself NSE allows 25%; the international standard and the big US index company allow 30%. On the same yardstick, a company at 27% would pass one and fail the other
Averaging Some index companies average market value over 24 months, some over 36; a screen that uses a single day's figure moves with every swing in the share price
Extra tests NSE adds a test on cash plus money owed to the company; the big US index company adds one on cash plus interest-bearing securities. A company can pass the debt test and fail one of these
How often the list is updated NSE reviews its Shariah indices every month and removes any company its Shariah board finds non-compliant; others work to their own schedules, so two lists checked on the same day can reflect different months

This site uses NSE's list because the exchange publishes it every month, dated, where anyone can check it.
