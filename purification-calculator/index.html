How much should you give away to purify your shares?

When a halal company earns some interest, you are expected to give away your share of it to charity: this is called purification. There are three common ways to work out your share, and they can differ a lot. One takes a slice of your dividend; the other two take your share of all the interest the company earned that year.

On this page
  1. Work it out for your shares
  2. The three methods, and who uses them
  3. Where to find the numbers

Work it out for your shares

Take a made-up company. This year it earned ₹10,000 crore in total, ₹120 crore of it as interest. It has 50 crore shares and paid a dividend of ₹6 a share. You held 100 of its shares for the whole year.

Put in any company's numbers and the table below updates.

The amount to give away under three purification methods
Method You would give
Dividend method A slice of your dividend. Used by a big US index company's Shariah indices ₹7.20
Year-end method Your share of the year's interest, if you still held the shares at the company's year-end. The international Shariah standard's method ₹240.00
Months-held method The same, scaled to the months you held them. Adopted by the Shariah board that screens NSE's indices ₹240.00

This covers the interest the company earned, not profit you make from selling shares.

With the starting numbers, the dividend method asks for ₹7.20 and the other two for ₹240 — over 30 times more. The dividend method only purifies the part of the interest that reached you as a dividend. The other two count your share of all of it, paid out or not.

The three methods, and who uses them

The three formulas below are as set out by the Shariah board that screens NSE's Shariah indices.

1. Dividend method

Interest income ÷ total income × dividend per share × shares you hold. A big US index company publishes this for its Shariah indices as the "dividend purification ratio". The Shariah board points out two weak spots: it gives away only a small part of the company's interest income, and in a year with no dividend, nothing at all.

What the sources say "Dividends * (Non-Permissible Revenue / Total Income) … This ratio is provided to investors for purification purposes and is not a compliance ratio." — The index company's Shariah index rules, September 2026 edition.

"It can be demonstrated that this method, in effect, leads to the purging of only a small part of the total interest income (from as little as under 5% to about 10% of the total interest income in the best case scenarios) … Also, in the event a company does not pay a dividend in a particular year, the interest income of that year is not purged at all." — The Shariah board, "Methodology of purging interest income".

2. Year-end method

Interest income ÷ the company's total shares × shares you hold at the company's year-end. This is the international Shariah standard's method. It counts your share of all the interest the company earned that year, whether it paid a dividend or not. The Shariah board's objection: it puts the whole year's purification on whoever happens to hold the shares on the last day of the company's financial year.

3. Months-held method — the one NSE's Shariah board uses

The year-end amount × months you held the shares ÷ 12. It spreads the purification over the time you actually held the shares, so selling just before the year-end doesn't avoid it, and buying just before doesn't load you with the whole year. The Shariah board says it has adopted this version, and calls it "logical and equitable".

What the Shariah board says On the year-end method: "there appears to be a tenuous justification for laying the responsibility of purging the interest for the entire year solely and entirely on the person who is found to be holding the shares at the time of closing of the financial year."

On the months-held method: "This method requires the purging of impure (interest) income earned per share of a company held by an investor pro-rated over the period for which the investor held those shares in his portfolio … The modified [year-end] method of purification is logical and equitable." — The Shariah board, "Methodology of purging interest income".

NSE's own rules ask investors to purge "the pro rata portion of interest income accrued on their holding of shares", which reads closest to the second and third methods.

Where to find the numbers

  1. Total income and interest income — in the company's annual report, in the statement of profit and loss. "Total income" has a line of its own. Interest income sits inside "Other income": open the note that line points to. Take both figures from the same statement — reports usually have a standalone and a consolidated version.
  2. Total shares — the "Equity share capital" note in the same annual report gives the number of shares.
  3. Dividend per share — the dividend declared for that year, in the annual report or the company's announcement to the stock exchange.
  4. Your shares and months held — your broker's holdings statement.

Enter money in ₹ crore, as most Indian annual reports print it. If a report prints ₹ lakh or ₹ million instead, convert first: 1 crore is 100 lakh, or 10 million.
